The U.S.–Italy Social Security (totalization) Agreement decides which country’s old-age, disability and survivors insurance covers a worker when U.S. and Italian rules would otherwise both apply. For remote and cross-border setups, the key documents are the certificate of coverage and the Agreement’s Article 7 rules on applicable laws.

This explainer summarises the [SSA pamphlet on the Agreement with Italy](https://www.ssa.gov/international/Agreement_Pamphlets/italy.html) and the [official Agreement text](https://www.ssa.gov/international/Agreement_Texts/italy.html). For broader mobility issues see our [cross-border employment checklist](/blog/cross-border-employment/).

## TL;DR

* Agreement in force since 1 November 1978 (supplementary agreement from 1 January 1986).
* Scope: Italian compulsory general insurance for old-age, disability and survivors; U.S. Title II Social Security (with stated exceptions).
* Default rule: coverage follows the territory where the work is performed, unless Article 7 exemptions or elections apply.
* Certificates of coverage (including IT/USA 4 for Italian-side claims of U.S. exemption) prove which system applies.
* Totalization can combine periods of coverage for benefit eligibility; it does not set tax residence or immigration status.

## What the Agreement is for

Without a totalization agreement, a person working across the United States and Italy can face **dual social security coverage**—paying into both systems for the same work—or gaps that block benefit eligibility. The Agreement:

* assigns **one** applicable legislation in the situations it covers;
* lets each country, when needed, **count the other country’s periods of coverage** toward eligibility;
* provides for mutual administrative assistance and certificates that employers and the self-employed can show auditors.

SSA states that Italian coverage under the Agreement means compulsory general insurance for old-age, disability and survivors (plus substitute benefits). U.S. coverage means Title II of the Social Security Act and related regulations, excluding sections 226, 226A and 228 and their regulations.

## Applicable-law rules that matter for remote work

Article 7 starts from a territory rule: persons employed or self-employed in one Contracting State’s territory are subject to that State’s laws, **except** as the Article provides.

Special rules then address nationals and dual coverage. In the official text:

* Services by a **U.S. national in Italy** that are covered under U.S. law remain covered under U.S. law.
* Services by an **Italian national in the United States** for an Italian employer or an enterprise controlled by an Italian firm are covered under Italian law.
* Where the same period of work would be subject to **both** laws, nationality and election rules decide which system remains applicable, and the other is waived once the covering agency **certifies** coverage.

SSA’s self-employment summary is consistent with that structure: self-employed U.S. nationals working in the United States and Italy are assigned U.S. coverage; certain Italian or dual nationals may elect. Always match the fact pattern to Article 7 and current SSA/INPS instructions before assuming an outcome.

## Certificates of coverage in practice

To show exemption from the other country’s system, you need a certificate:

* **Italian certificate for employees (IT/USA 4):** the employer in Italy requests it from the provincial INPS office where the employer is located. SSA lists the data to include (identity, citizenship, residence, SSN, employers, dates of hire/transfer).
* **U.S. employers** should retain certificates for possible IRS audit; do not send a copy to the IRS unless requested.
* **Self-employed** Italian or dual nationals who elect Italian coverage write to the provincial INPS office and should attach the certificate to the U.S. tax return when claiming exemption.

Without a certificate, dual-contribution disputes are harder to resolve even when the Agreement would support one system.

## Benefits and what totalization does not do

If you have credits in both countries, you may qualify for a regular benefit from one or both systems. When one system alone is short of credits, the Agreement can let that country count the other country’s periods (with minima: for example, SSA notes at least six U.S. credits before Italian credits can be counted for a U.S. benefit).

The Agreement does **not**:

* replace Italian INPS obligations outside the covered branches;
* decide Italian or U.S. **tax residence**;
* create a visa, remote-work permit or immigration status;
* totalize Medicare entitlement with Italian credits (Medicare-related Title II sections are excluded from the Agreement’s U.S. scope as listed by SSA).

## FAQ

### What does the US-Italy totalization agreement cover?

SSA states that for Italy it covers compulsory general insurance for old-age, disability and survivors, including substitute benefits; for the United States, Title II of the Social Security Act with the listed exceptions.

### Which country’s social security applies when I work in one territory?

Article 7’s default is that people employed or self-employed in one Contracting State’s territory are subject to that State’s laws, subject to the Article’s special rules and exemptions.

### What if I would otherwise be covered by both systems?

Article 7 uses nationality-based and election rules. Exemptions take effect when the agency of the covering State certifies coverage to the other agency.

### How do certificates of coverage work?

An Italian employer seeking U.S. exemption requests IT/USA 4 from the provincial INPS office. Self-employed electors follow SSA’s written request process and keep the certificate with U.S. filings when claiming exemption.

### Does totalization decide tax residence or immigration status?

No. It allocates social security coverage and can help with benefit eligibility. Tax residence and immigration remain separate.
