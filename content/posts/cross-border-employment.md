Cross-border employment is any arrangement where a person works in a country other than the one where their employer is based or where they usually live. That covers a relocation, a short assignment, a posting to a client, a hire in another country and a remote worker who moves abroad. Each country the work touches can claim a say over tax, immigration, social security and employment terms. The risk rarely comes from one big rule. It comes from small facts nobody checked: where the person sits, who pays them, how long they stay, and which paperwork was never filed.

This guide is for individuals planning a move and for the professionals and HR teams who manage global workforce mobility. It explains what triggers obligations, what to document, and how to review a case before it becomes expensive.


## TL;DR

* Working in another country can trigger immigration, tax, social-security and employment-law duties at the same time, often in two countries.
* Presence, duration and who pays are the facts that decide most outcomes. Record them from day one.
* Social-security coverage usually follows specific rules or bilateral agreements, and proof of coverage is a document you request, not an assumption.
* Employers can create a taxable presence abroad through an employee's activities, even without opening an office.
* A structured review that lists known facts, missing facts and sources beats a generic checklist.

## What cross-border employment means in practice

The term covers several different situations, and they carry different rules. Mixing them up is the first mistake.

| Situation | Typical example | Main questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Permanent relocation | An employee transfers to a foreign office | Work permit, local payroll, tax residency |
| Temporary assignment or posting | An employer sends staff to serve a client abroad for a set period | Host-country terms, social-security certificate, notification |
| Cross-border hire | A company hires someone who lives abroad | Local employment law, employer registration, contractor or employee status |
| Remote work from abroad | An employee works from another country for weeks or months | Tax presence, right to work, insurance |
| Commuter work | Living in one country and working in the next | Tax split, social-security state, benefits |

Global workforce mobility is the umbrella term companies use for managing all of these. Global mobility risk management is the discipline of finding the problems in each case before the move starts rather than after the first payslip is wrong.

## When working abroad creates obligations

Four areas move together. Check each, because clearing one does not clear the others.

### Immigration and the right to work

A person relocating for a genuine work assignment generally needs visa sponsorship if they have no existing legal right to work in the host country, and must meet the requirements of the relevant permit category, according to [a global mobility best-practices paper from the San Jose State University Tax Institute](https://www.sjsu.edu/taxinstitute/docs/3E_GlobalMobilityBestPractices_11-5-24.pdf). A tourist or visitor status that is fine for a meeting is usually not fine for productive work. Do not let anyone start working on arrival without confirming the permit category in writing.

### Income tax and taxable presence

The OECD Model Tax Convention is the template behind many bilateral tax treaties. Under it, [the country where the work is done may tax employment income](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/events/public-consultations/2025/11/global-mobility-of-individuals/public-consultation-document-global-mobility-of-individuals.pdf) if the employee is present there for more than 183 days, if an employer resident there pays the remuneration, or if a permanent establishment there bears the cost. The actual treaty between the two countries controls, so the 183-day figure is a starting point and not a safe harbor. Counting days across a rolling period, and noting who pays and who bears the cost, is basic record-keeping.

The employer has its own exposure. The same OECD document notes that an employee's physical presence [can give rise to a taxable presence for the enterprise](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/events/public-consultations/2025/11/global-mobility-of-individuals/public-consultation-document-global-mobility-of-individuals.pdf), including a permanent establishment, although activities that are only preparatory or auxiliary may prevent one. The OECD's 2025 update to the Model Tax Convention [addresses cross-border home-office arrangements](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2025/11/oecd-updates-model-tax-convention-to-reflect-rise-of-cross-border-remote-work-and-clarify-taxation-of-natural-resources.html) and aims to give businesses more tax certainty. Whether a particular remote arrangement creates a permanent establishment still depends on the treaty and the facts, which is why the role of the person abroad should be described in writing: do they sign contracts, manage a team, or only support work done elsewhere?

Payroll withholding is separate again. A UK employer, for instance, [must keep calculating and deducting PAYE income tax](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/paying-employees-working-abroad) for employees working abroad, while National Insurance depends on the destination country and the applicable rules. When an employee returns after a stint abroad for a UK employer, [HMRC should be notified so the tax code can be reviewed](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/paye-manual/paye81545). Every country has its own version of these steps.

### Social security

Social-security duties surprise people most, because they run on different logic from income tax. For US workers, the IRS explains that Social Security and Medicare taxes [generally continue to apply to wages for services performed abroad](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/social-security-tax-consequences-of-working-abroad) when the employee works for an American employer or another listed condition applies. The same page says totalization agreements remove dual coverage and contributions for the same work.

The Social Security Administration lists [the countries the United States has totalization agreements with](https://www.ssa.gov/international/agreement_descriptions.html), including Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, South Korea, Australia and many EU members. If your destination is not on that list, double contributions are a live possibility and need to be planned for.

The principle is global. An International Labour Organization paper describes bilateral social-security agreements as [the most favored approach to worker mobility](https://researchrepository.ilo.org/esploro/fulltext/book/Are-social-security-agreements-between-India/995218631802676?institution=41ILO_INST&mId=13100886190002676&repId=12100530710002676), because they coordinate national schemes, avoid dual contributions and help protect workers' rights.

Inside the EU, Regulation 987/2009 says an employer established outside the competent member state must meet the obligations of that state's legislation, [including paying contributions](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2009/987/title/II/adopted/data.xht?view=snippet&wrap=true), as if it were established there.

### Employment terms

Host countries often protect people who work on their soil. For EU postings, [posted workers must generally receive the host country's terms and conditions of employment](https://europa.eu/youreurope/business/human-resources/cross-border-posted-workers/posting-staff-abroad/index_en.htm), and if their home-country terms are more favorable, those should be maintained. Minimum pay, working time, leave and notice rules can all differ from the contract signed at home.

## The risks people miss

Most failures come from a small set of repeat patterns. This is a working list drawn from the rules above, not a count of how often each occurs.

1. **Work starts before the permit is in place.** The person is already there, so the fix is expensive.
2. **Days are not counted.** No one can show whether a treaty threshold was crossed, so the answer defaults to the worst case.
3. **Coverage is assumed.** Nobody requested the certificate that proves which country's social security applies.
4. **The employer's presence is ignored.** A senior employee abroad who negotiates deals can change the company's tax position.
5. **Payroll runs in one country only.** Withholding, reporting and contributions are owed in a country where no payroll exists.
6. **Contract terms don't match local law.** Notice, leave or pay terms fall below the host-country floor.
7. **Temporary becomes permanent.** A six-month stay is extended twice and no one revisits the analysis.
8. **No record.** When a tax authority asks later, the file has emails and nothing else.

The last point matters more than it looks. Even the OECD's COVID-era analysis, which said exceptional and temporary location changes should not create new permanent establishments, was paired in Ireland with guidance to [keep records of the facts and circumstances](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2020/04/oecd-secretariat-analysis-of-tax-treaties-and-the-impact-of-the-covid-19-crisis_6d6eac80/947dcb01-en.pdf) if the presence resulted from travel restrictions. Relief often depends on being able to prove the facts.

## Documents and records to keep

For an EU posting, the official guidance says to [request a Portable Document A1, notify the host-country authorities before the posting starts](https://europa.eu/youreurope/business/human-resources/cross-border-posted-workers/posting-staff-abroad/index_en.htm), and give workers posted for more than four consecutive weeks written information about the host country, expected duration, pay and related terms. Outside the EU the forms differ, but the file should look similar.

A workable minimum for any cross-border case:

* The employment contract or assignment letter, with start date, expected end date and pay terms.
* Proof of the right to work: visa, permit or statement of the status relied on.
* Proof of social-security coverage, such as a certificate under an agreement or a registration in the host country.
* A day-count log showing where the person worked each day.
* A short description of what the person actually does abroad.
* Payroll records showing which entity paid, where and in what currency.
* Copies of notifications or registrations filed with host-country authorities.
* A dated list of sources relied on, such as the treaty article, agency page or guidance, so a later reviewer can see why a decision was made.

## How individuals and employers can manage the risk

### For individuals

Start with three questions: which country will tax my income, which social-security system will I pay into, and what legal status lets me work there? If you cannot answer each in one sentence, you have a missing fact. Ask your employer in writing, keep the answers, and keep your own day log. Do not rely on a colleague's experience in a different country or at a different time.

### For employers and HR teams

1. **Classify the case.** Relocation, posting, local hire, remote work or commuter: each starts a different checklist.
2. **Map the four areas.** Immigration, tax, social security and employment terms, for both home and host country.
3. **Identify missing facts.** Expected duration, who signs contracts, where the person's family lives, who pays and who bears the cost.
4. **Set review triggers.** Extension of the assignment, a change of role, or a change of residence should each reopen the file.
5. **Keep an audit trail.** Record who decided what, on which source, and when.

This is also what a global mobility risk assessment or a workforce mobility review should produce: a clear picture of which cases carry risk, what is still unknown, and what to check next. Where a case involves a treaty position, a permanent establishment question or a visa category, bring in a tax adviser or immigration lawyer licensed in the relevant country before the move, not after.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between cross-border employment and global mobility?

Cross-border employment describes the work arrangement itself: someone working in a country other than their employer's or their home country. Global mobility is the function, usually inside a company, that manages those arrangements, including relocations, assignments and remote work abroad. In practice the terms overlap, and both involve immigration, tax and social-security questions.

### Can an employee work remotely from another country without creating obligations?

Not automatically. Immigration status, personal tax, social-security coverage and the employer's own tax position can all be affected, depending on the countries, the duration and the role. Check each area for the specific destination and document the outcome before the person starts.

### Do I pay social security in two countries if I work abroad?

Sometimes. A [US totalization agreement](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/social-security-tax-consequences-of-working-abroad) is designed to eliminate dual coverage for the same work, but only for countries that have one with the United States. Ask for proof of which system covers you, and confirm the rule for your specific country pair.

### How long can someone work abroad before taxes apply?

There is no single number. Under the OECD Model many treaties use a 183-day test, but the source country may also tax if a local employer pays or bears the cost, and domestic rules can apply earlier. Read the actual treaty and count days with a log.

### What documents should I request before an international assignment?

Ask for a written assignment letter, confirmation of your work permit category, proof of social-security coverage, and a note on who runs payroll and where. Keep your own record of days worked in each country.
